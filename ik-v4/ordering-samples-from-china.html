Ordering Samples from China: What to Ask For, Timing and Cost

A sample is the cheapest point at which to find out a finish is wrong. Here is what to request, how to plan the time, which costs to expect and how to make an approval stick through production.

Ordering samples: quick answer

Ask for the sample that answers your biggest risk: a finish swatch for colour, a corner or door sample for construction, or a full product when comfort, size or assembly matters. Get the sample price, making time and delivery cost in writing before you pay. Label and photograph what you approve, then attach it to the order specification so the production goods are checked against it.

Why order a sample at all?

Photographs and showroom pictures hide the things that cause most disputes: the exact shade of an oak veneer, the sheen of a painted door, how firm a seat feels, or whether a drawer runner closes smoothly. A sample settles those questions while changing them still costs little.

It also gives everyone the same reference. Once you approve a sample, the factory, the inspector and you are all working to one physical object rather than a description open to interpretation. Choose the right type of sample

Not every product needs a full sample. Match the sample to what you need to check.

Finish swatches
Board, laminate, paint, veneer, fabric or stone pieces. Good for colour and texture across a whole scheme.
Construction samples
A cabinet corner, a door with hinges fitted or a section of a wardrobe. Shows the carcass board, edging and hardware.
Full product samples
One chair, one bed frame or one light fitting. Worth it when comfort, proportion or assembly matters.
Pre-production sample
A unit made to your final specification before the main run. Useful for bespoke or repeat items.

For a kitchen, a door in your chosen finish plus a corner sample showing the carcass, edging and hinge is usually more useful than a whole cabinet. Our guide to checking kitchen cabinets from China explains what to look at on that corner.

For loose furniture, one full chair or bedside table tells you far more than a brochure. For a scheme across many rooms, a single board of swatches showing every finish side by side helps you check they work together.

What to ask for when you request a sample

Send a short written request rather than a chat message. It should state:

  • The product reference from the quotation and the exact finish, fabric or colour reference.
  • The dimensions and any changes from the standard product.
  • Whether the sample is made from the same materials and process as the production goods.
  • The hardware to be fitted: hinges, runners, handles, legs or castors.
  • The sample price, the making time and the delivery cost, each confirmed in writing.
  • Whether the sample cost is deducted from a later order, if the supplier offers that.

Ask the factory to label the sample with the product reference and the date. When you have several swatches of similar colours, unlabelled pieces cause confusion quickly.

Ask for compliance evidence at the same time

A sample is a good point to see the paperwork the production goods will come with. For upholstered furniture, ask how the permanent fire label will look and what test evidence supports it. The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations apply to many upholstered items supplied in the UK, so confirm which products in your order are in scope before you approve.

For mattresses and beds, ask for the BS 7177 evidence. For lighting and other electrical goods, ask for the UKCA or CE conformity documents. A sample that looks right but arrives without the evidence you need is not ready to approve.

How long sampling takes

There is no single answer, because three separate stages add together:

  1. Making the sample. A swatch cut from existing stock can be quick. A sample in a custom size or finish has to be made, which takes longer.
  2. Getting it to the UK. Small samples usually travel by courier or air. Large items cost more to send this way and may need a different arrangement.
  3. Customs and delivery. The sample has to clear UK customs before the courier can deliver it.

Ask the factory for a sample-ready date in writing and plan around it. Then allow time for your own review. If several people need to see a sample, agree who has the final say before it arrives, or approval can stall for weeks.

Sampling happens before production, so it sits outside the main order window. IK ENG's standard items typically take 10–12 weeks to site, and bespoke items 12–18 weeks from approved drawings. If your room-ready date is fixed, count back from that date and make sure samples are approved in time for the order to start.

What samples cost

Sample costs vary with the product and how it travels, so get each line quoted rather than assuming. Expect some or all of these:

  • The sample itself. Factories often charge more for a single piece than the unit price on a production order, because set-up costs fall on one item.
  • Courier or air freight. Often the largest part of the cost for anything bigger than a swatch.
  • Import VAT and duty. A sample is still an import. Import VAT and, depending on the product's commodity code, customs duty may be due. Check the code on the UK Trade Tariff and read the gov.uk guidance on importing goods into the UK before relying on a figure.
  • Courier handling charges. Couriers may add a fee for clearing customs and collecting tax on your behalf. Ask about this before you book.

Ask for sample costs to sit on their own lines, separate from the production quotation. That way you can see what the sampling stage costs and compare it fairly between factories.

Check the sample properly when it arrives

Unpack it carefully and keep the packaging; it tells you how the production goods are likely to be packed. Then check against your written request:

  • Measure the key dimensions and compare them with the drawing.
  • Look at colour and sheen in daylight and under the lighting the room will have.
  • Open doors and drawers repeatedly. Check hinges, runners and handles for movement and noise.
  • Look at edges, joints and the back and underside, not just the front face.
  • Check labels, care information and any compliance documents.

Note anything you want changed and send it back in writing. If the changes are significant, ask for a revised sample rather than approving a description of the change.

Make the approval count in production

An approved sample only protects you if production is checked against it. Photograph the sample in good light with its label visible, record the date and keep the physical piece. Add the sample reference to the order specification so it is part of what you are paying for.
