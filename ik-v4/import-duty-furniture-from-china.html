Import Duty and VAT on Furniture from China (UK Guide)

A factory price is only one part of the budget. Before ordering furniture from China, establish the product classification, customs value, taxes and delivery scope. This guide focuses on imports into Great Britain and flags where Northern Ireland needs a separate check.

Start with the goods, not a headline duty rate

“Furniture” is too broad a description for a reliable customs estimate. A wooden dining table, upholstered seat, metal cabinet and lighting fitting can have different classifications. Break a whole-house order into product lines and collect the material, function, construction and intended use for each one.

Ask suppliers for clear descriptions and product references before shipment. Keep the invoice, packing list and order schedule consistent. A container containing several room packs is still a collection of different goods; calling everything a furniture package does not remove the need to classify the contents properly.

Find the commodity code in the official tariff

Use the official UK Trade Tariff to check the commodity code, origin, import date and applicable measures. Do not rely on an old online table or a code copied from a different product. Your customs agent can help resolve uncertain classifications.

This guide does not quote a fixed furniture duty rate. The tariff result for the exact goods is the useful starting point. Save the classification information with the product schedule so the person preparing the declaration can see what was checked and why the description fits.

Understand customs value

Customs duty is calculated using the relevant customs value, not automatically the factory's product total alone. The value normally includes the goods and applicable transport and insurance costs to the UK border, with adjustments where required. HMRC explains the approach in its transaction-value guidance.

For planning, ask for a breakdown of factory goods, inland transport, export handling, international freight and insurance. Keep each charge visible so the agent can work out what belongs in the customs value. This also prevents a delivered cost from being compared with another supplier's factory-only price.

Import VAT uses a wider calculation

The import VAT value starts from the customs value and includes relevant duty and incidental expenses to the first UK destination, plus a further destination where it is known at import. Check the details in HMRC's import VAT valuation guidance.

In a budget sheet, keep customs value, duty, VAT value and import VAT as separate lines. Avoid applying VAT only to the factory price or adding international freight twice. Ask your customs agent to reconcile the calculation to the actual shipment and delivery arrangement before you approve the final figures.

A calculation framework without invented rates

Start with the customs value agreed for the declaration. Apply the applicable tariff treatment for each product line to determine duty. Build the import VAT value using that customs value, the relevant duty and the additional costs required by HMRC. Then apply the VAT treatment that applies to those goods.

The result feeds into the total project budget alongside product cost, sourcing fee and other services. This is a framework, not a substitute for a declaration calculation. Use the live tariff and current shipment charges to populate it, rather than treating a sample percentage as the rate for your order.

Who is the importer, and who pays?

Agree the importer of record and the delivery terms before placing the order. Ask whose details will appear on the customs entry and who will arrange payment or accounting for duty and VAT. A freight forwarder may submit paperwork, but that does not mean the forwarder owns every responsibility attached to the import.

Request a written cost breakdown stating whether the quotation includes customs clearance, duty, import VAT and final delivery. If a supplier uses “delivered” without defining the scope, ask for the destination and included charges. Do this while comparing quotations, when changing the purchasing arrangement is still straightforward.

VAT registration does not make the question disappear

Eligible UK VAT-registered businesses may use postponed VAT accounting, recording import VAT through their VAT return instead of paying it at the border. The normal recovery rules still apply. HMRC sets out eligibility and instructions in its postponed VAT accounting guidance.

Do not assume every property buyer can recover all VAT. Tell your accountant what the goods will be used for and who will import them. Ask how taxes should appear in the cash-flow forecast and which documents are needed. A planning budget should show the actual cash requirement and any later accounting treatment separately.

Keep the shipment documents together

Build one file for the order: approved schedule, commercial invoice, packing list, freight charges, insurance, transport documents and customs entry information. Match quantities and descriptions before departure. HMRC's valuation documentation guidance lists examples of supporting records.

Give the broker a contact who can answer product questions quickly. Photos and technical descriptions can help explain an unfamiliar item. For mixed orders, preserve individual product references even if goods are consolidated into one shipment. This makes both classification and later stock allocation easier to follow.

Plan final delivery as a separate task

Clearing customs does not put furniture into rooms. Confirm the delivery postcode, vehicle access, unloading arrangements, storage and receiving contact. Check whether assembly, stair access or specialist handling is part of the price. Record these services rather than assuming the last delivery line includes them all.

For phased schemes, label goods by property or unit and check how each delivery stage affects the logistics plan. Our furniture packs for property developers and landlord furniture packages start with those room and property schedules.

Northern Ireland and your next quotation

Goods imported into Northern Ireland can follow different customs and VAT arrangements. Give the actual destination at the enquiry stage and ask the broker to check the route. Do not reuse a Great Britain calculation unchanged for a County Down or Belfast delivery.
