Fitted Wardrobes from China: Measuring, Drawings and What the Factory Needs

A fitted wardrobe is only as good as the measurements and drawings behind it. This guide covers how to measure the space, what to put on the drawings and what a factory needs before it can quote and make your units.

The short version

Measure every opening in several places, record everything that interrupts the wall, decide the internal layout before the doors, and send the factory one approved drawing set with a revision number. Bespoke wardrobes typically take 12–18 weeks from approved drawings, so the drawings are the real start of the programme.

Why measuring matters more when the factory is overseas

A local joiner can visit, scribe a panel on site and cut a filler to suit. A factory in China cannot. It makes exactly what is on the drawing, packs it and ships it. If the alcove is 12mm narrower at the top than at the bottom, or a socket sits where a drawer box should go, you find out on installation day.

That does not make bespoke wardrobes from China risky. It means the work moves earlier. Time spent measuring properly and checking drawings is time you do not spend waiting for a replacement panel to cross the sea.

How to measure the space

Use a laser measure and a long spirit level, and write every figure on a sketch of the room as you take it. Work in millimetres throughout. The factory will.

  1. Width at three heights. Measure wall to wall at the floor, at roughly waist height and just below the ceiling. Walls are rarely parallel. Record all three and note which is smallest.
  2. Height at three points. Measure floor to ceiling at the left, centre and right. Floors and ceilings slope more often than people expect, especially in older houses and conversions.
  3. Depth. Measure the usable depth from the back wall to where the doors will sit. For hanging space, allow enough depth for a coat hanger plus clearance; ask for the factory's recommended minimum for the door type you choose.
  4. Out of square and out of plumb. Check corners with the level and note how far each wall leans over the full height. This tells the designer how much filler or scribe allowance to build in.
  5. Sloping ceilings. For loft rooms, measure the height of the knee wall, where the slope starts, and the height at the front of the proposed units. A photo with a tape measure in shot helps.

If the room is still being plastered or the flooring is not down, measure again once it is. Final floor finishes change heights, and fresh plasterboard can move a wall line by more than you think.

Record everything on the walls

Wardrobes rarely go onto a clean, empty wall. Mark the position and size of each of these on your sketch, measured from a fixed corner:

Skirting height and depth, and whether it will be removed or scribed around
Coving, picture rails and cornice
Sockets, switches, thermostats and data points
Radiators and pipework
Windows, window boards and curtain clearance
Room doors and their swing
Loft hatches, boxing-in and soil stacks
Any existing lighting or cable you want to reuse

Then check the delivery route. Measure the front door, hallway turns, stair width and headroom on the landing. Tall carcasses that will not go up the stairs need splitting into smaller units on the drawing, not on site.

Plan the inside before the doors

Most disappointment with fitted wardrobes comes from the interior, not the finish. Write down what each wardrobe needs to hold, then let that set the layout.

  • Long hanging for coats and dresses. Note the length of the longest item you need to hang.
  • Double hanging for shirts and jackets, with one rail above another.
  • Drawers, including how many, how deep, and whether any need to sit behind a hinged door (which affects hinge and drawer clearance).
  • Shelving, fixed or adjustable, and the spacing you want.
  • Shoe storage, pull-out trays, trouser racks or pull-down rails for high units.
  • Integrated lighting, and where the power will come from.

If you have a particular style in mind, reference photos and a list of the compartments you want are far more useful to a factory than a style name. For rental property, keep interiors simple and robust: fewer moving parts, standard shelf depths and hardware that is easy to replace.

Doors, finishes and hardware

Decide early between hinged and sliding doors. Sliding doors save floor space in tight bedrooms but need extra depth for the track and give you access to only part of the wardrobe at once. Hinged doors need clear space in front to swing open, so check them against beds, radiators and the room door.

On the specification, give the factory:

  • The board and finish for carcasses and doors, with a sample or colour reference agreed in advance.
  • Edge banding on every visible edge, and which edges are visible.
  • Hinge type and whether doors are soft-close.
  • Drawer runner type, extension and soft-close requirement.
  • Handle style, size and position, or push-to-open if you want handleless doors.
  • Mirror panels, glass or any special inserts.

Many of the same carcass and hardware checks apply to kitchens. Our guide to what to check on kitchen cabinets from China goes into board, edging and hinges in more detail.

What goes on the drawings

A factory needs drawings it can manufacture from without guessing. For each wardrobe, the drawing set should include:

  1. A plan view showing the room, the wardrobe footprint, door swings and the fixed reference corner.
  2. Front elevations of each wall with overall sizes, each unit width, door divisions and handle positions.
  3. Internal elevations showing rails, shelves and drawers with heights, so the interior matches what you planned.
  4. Sections where depth changes, around sloping ceilings or where units meet skirting and coving.
  5. Filler, plinth and top panel sizes, plus how they will be scribed to the wall and ceiling.
  6. A unit schedule listing every carcass, door, drawer and panel with its size, finish and room reference.

Give the set a revision number and keep one person responsible for approving changes. When two people send corrections to the factory separately, the wrong version tends to get made.

From approved drawings to installation

Once the drawings and finishes are approved, production can start. Bespoke items typically take 12–18 weeks from approved drawings to site, covering manufacture, packing, sea freight, customs and delivery. Late changes to a width or a door finish can affect several components, so ask about the effect on the programme before approving one. Our China to UK shipping times guide breaks down each stage.

Ask for every carton to be labelled by property, room and unit number to match the schedule. It makes installation far quicker, especially on multi-unit projects. Before the final balance is paid, check the factory video of the finished units against your drawings: carcass sizes, door finish, handles and internal fittings.

Book your fitter with the drawings in hand. Installation and site templating are arranged separately, so your fitter should see the drawings before the order is placed, not after it arrives.
