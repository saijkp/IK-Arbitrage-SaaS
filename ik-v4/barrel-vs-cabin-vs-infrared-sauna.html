Barrel, Cabin or Infrared Sauna: Which Should You Choose?

Choose by available space, the way you want the heat to feel and how the sauna will reach its position. Barrel and cabin describe the structure, while infrared describes the heating system, so confirm the complete specification rather than relying on a listing category.

Barrel saunas: check the curve and the joints

A barrel uses shaped timber staves held together around a curved shell, commonly with external bands. Confirm the timber species, grade, stave thickness, joints, supports and roof covering. A curved roof alone does not establish how the unit keeps rain out.

Allow a level, load-bearing base with support positions matched to the drawings, plus clearance for the door, steps and maintenance. Ask for bench dimensions and usable headroom for the intended number of people. An electric heater or wood stove can be specified where the design permits, with its required clearances.

A traditional heater heats the air and surrounding surfaces. Warm-up depends on heater output, timber, weather and glazing; ask for model-specific instructions rather than a universal time. A kit needs stave assembly and alignment, while a pre-built barrel can require lifting equipment and a wide access route.

Cabin saunas: layout and construction choices

A rectangular cabin can offer flexible bench lengths and levels, useful when comparing seating for several people. Confirm the measured seating plan rather than a capacity label. Construction may use solid timber boards or framed, insulated panels with timber lining; record wall thickness, insulation and the intended indoor or outdoor specification.

Traditional cabins use an electric heater or a wood-burning stove where suitable. They heat the air; glazing, insulation and room volume influence warm-up and how evenly the space heats. Indoor installation needs the manufacturer's room, ventilation and clearance requirements checked. Outdoor cabins need a weatherproof roof and a suitable base.

Panel kits need assembly space and careful joints; pre-built cabins need delivery and lifting access for the full external dimensions. A larger rectangular floor plan may suit a dedicated garden area or a gym, spa or hotel, provided the model and installation are specified for the intended use.

Infrared cabins: panels and seating positions

Infrared panels deliver radiant heat towards the occupants and surfaces, so the sensation differs from a traditional cabin heated mainly through hot air. Air temperatures and warm-up instructions vary by model. Compare the panel positions with where each person will sit, without treating the systems as interchangeable.

Check timber species, panel construction, floor requirements and the documented number of seats. Many infrared cabins are intended for indoor rooms; do not put an indoor model outdoors under a cover unless the manufacturer explicitly allows that installation.

Infrared cabins often arrive as panels in a crate. Check that every panel fits through doors and around corners, and allow space to assemble and service the unit. Their electrical load can be lower than a traditional heater, but the supply must still follow the manual.

Combination infrared and traditional units

Some cabins combine infrared panels with a traditional heater. Confirm whether the systems can run together, the controller instructions, clearances and the total electrical demand for each permitted mode. Ask for the same construction and documentation detail as for either separate system.

Match the choice to the user and site

  • Homeowners: compare the available garden or indoor floor space, seating layout, preferred heating system and access route.
  • Holiday lets: check guest capacity, clear operating instructions, cleaning access and commercial-use warranty terms, alongside planning and installation advice.
  • Gyms, spas and hotels: specify expected occupancy, operating hours, controls, maintenance access and suitability for commercial use before comparing models.

Check delivery and installation before choosing

Ask for packed pallet or crate dimensions, weight and the longest component, then measure gates, doors, turns and overhead obstructions. Agree unloading equipment and who moves the goods from the delivery point to the base. Assembly, electrical work and any stove installation need named installers and a clear scope.

Frequently asked questions

Is a barrel sauna always quicker to heat?

No. Warm-up depends on the heater, cabin volume, construction, glazing and ambient conditions. Compare the instructions for the exact models rather than assuming one shape is faster.

Can an infrared cabin go outdoors?

Only choose outdoor installation where the manufacturer explicitly specifies that model for it. Many infrared cabins are designed for indoor rooms.

How should I compare the number of people?

Ask for bench lengths, seating positions and usable headroom on a dimensioned drawing. Check these against the intended users rather than relying only on a capacity label.

Can one cabin include infrared and a traditional heater?

Yes, combination units exist. Confirm the permitted operating modes, controls, electrical demand and installation clearances for the exact model.
