How to calculate CBM for furniture shipping

Calculate packed volume from outside carton dimensions, then check weight and packing assumptions with your forwarder. The worked figures here are hypothetical examples, not quotations.

What CBM measures

CBM means cubic metre: a measure of the space a shipment occupies. To calculate CBM for furniture shipping, use the outside dimensions of the finished shipping carton, crate or pallet. The size of the furniture in a room is useful for planning installation, but it is not the measurement the forwarder needs for packed freight.

The formula for identical cartons is length in metres × width in metres × height in metres × number of cartons. Calculate each different carton size separately, then add the totals. Keep gross weight alongside volume: a small carton of dense fittings and a large lightweight panel pack can affect a freight quote differently.

Convert the units before multiplying

Divide centimetres by 100 to obtain metres, or millimetres by 1,000. A carton measuring 120 × 60 × 20 cm becomes 1.20 × 0.60 × 0.20 m. Multiplying centimetres directly produces cubic centimetres, not CBM. If you calculate entirely in centimetres, divide the product by 1,000,000 to obtain cubic metres.

Ask suppliers to state their units explicitly. A drawing may use millimetres while a packing list uses centimetres. Keep unrounded values during calculation and round only the displayed total; the forwarder may apply its own measurement and rounding rules. Confirm those rules before treating your estimate as a booking quantity.

Worked example: identical flat-pack cartons

Hypothetical example only: suppose ten furniture cartons each measure 120 × 60 × 20 cm externally. These are invented teaching dimensions, not an IK ENG product specification or shipment record.

  • Convert: 120 ÷ 100 = 1.20 m; 60 ÷ 100 = 0.60 m; 20 ÷ 100 = 0.20 m.
  • One carton: 1.20 × 0.60 × 0.20 = 0.144 CBM.
  • Ten cartons: 0.144 × 10 = 1.44 CBM.
  • Cross-check in centimetres: 120 × 60 × 20 × 10 ÷ 1,000,000 = 1.44 CBM.

The carton count matters. If one wardrobe comes in three different cartons, obtain all three sizes and quantities. Do not multiply the largest carton by the number of wardrobes unless every carton is actually that size. Ask the supplier to map each carton to an item code so missing boxes are easier to detect.

Flat-pack versus assembled furniture

Flat-pack furniture often occupies less space because panels can lie together. Assembled furniture includes the air inside the carcass as part of its outer shipping envelope. The difference depends on the design and protective packing, so compare actual proposed carton sizes rather than applying a standard saving percentage.

For illustration only, one hypothetical assembled carton measuring 0.80 × 0.60 × 0.70 m is 0.336 CBM. Two hypothetical flat packs each measuring 0.80 × 0.60 × 0.15 m total 0.144 CBM: 0.80 × 0.60 × 0.15 × 2. The difference is 0.192 CBM. This is a volume comparison, not proof that two real packing options would offer equal protection or contain the same product.

Include assembly time, fixing completeness, surface protection and site access in the decision. A smaller carton is not helpful if it increases damage or leaves installers without instructions. Ask for a packing proposal before comparing shipping options, especially for glass, stone tops, long panels or high-gloss finishes.

Packing and pallet allowance

Measure after corner protection, crates and other outer packaging have been fitted. If goods will travel on a pallet, ask for its complete loaded outside dimensions, including the pallet base and any overhang. Do not add carton CBM to the same pallet envelope: that would count the goods twice.

For a hypothetical loaded pallet measuring 1.20 × 1.00 × 1.30 m, the envelope is 1.56 CBM. That figure includes the space within the envelope, even if the cartons do not fill every gap. If the forwarder quotes loose-carton handling instead, send the loose-carton figures and explain the intended packing so the basis is clear.

There is no universal packing percentage to add. Use confirmed dimensions where available; label early allowances as estimates and replace them after packing. Tell the forwarder whether goods are stackable, fragile or restricted to a particular orientation. Non-stackable cargo may attract a different charging basis, so physical CBM alone may not describe the booked space.

How CBM affects LCL and FCL

LCL shares container space with other shipments. FCL reserves a container for your shipment. As the packed order grows, ask for both options rather than assuming a fixed CBM threshold decides the answer. Our LCL versus FCL guide explains the handling and booking differences.

Compare the complete service, including collection, packing, handling and delivery. A loading plan must consider carton shapes, door access, weight, stacking limits and protection, not simply divide a container volume by the total CBM. Some apparently spare space cannot be used without crushing or damaging furniture. Confirm the proposed equipment and loading plan with the forwarder.

CBM helps compare like-for-like quotes, but it does not create a freight price by itself. Use our China to UK shipping cost breakdown to check the scope of each quote. Keep the same carton list and packing assumptions when requesting alternatives.

Sea LCL: weight or measure

Sea LCL commonly uses a weight/measure basis comparing cubic metres with metric tonnes: commonly 1 CBM = 1,000 kg. Confirm the conversion, minimums, rounding and individual charge bases with your forwarder. DHL Global Forwarding explains the comparison between actual and volumetric weight; its sea LCL example uses this density relationship.

In a hypothetical comparison, 2.844 CBM weighing 900 kg is compared with 0.900 tonnes; volume is the larger measure. If the same volume instead weighs 3,200 kg, the weight measure is 3.200 tonnes and is larger than 2.844. These are charging-unit comparisons, not prices or a promise about every charge on a quote. Do not describe the converted figure as the physical weight of the goods.

Air freight uses a different calculation

Air volumetric weight is commonly length × width × height in centimetres ÷ 6,000, giving kilograms per package. Multiply by the number of identical packages and compare with actual gross weight. Carriers and services vary; confirm the divisor and rounding rules, particularly when comparing airline freight with express courier services.

Using the earlier hypothetical 120 × 60 × 20 cm carton, 120 × 60 × 20 ÷ 6,000 = 24 kg volumetric weight. Ten cartons give 240 kg. If their actual total gross weight is 180 kg, the volumetric figure is higher; at 300 kg actual gross weight, the actual figure is higher. Packaging is included in gross weight. The DHL guidance explains the commonly used air divisor, but the booked service terms govern.

What to send for a useful shipping quote

Send a packing list with supplier and item references, carton counts, external dimensions with units, gross weight per group, packing type and stackability. Include the goods-ready date and UK delivery postcode, plus site access and unloading details. If several suppliers are involved, keep their lists separate and provide a combined summary without double-counting cartons.

Frequently asked questions

Do I measure the furniture or its carton?

Use the outside dimensions of the final shipping carton, crate or loaded pallet. Product dimensions alone exclude protective packing.

Can I add different carton sizes together?

Calculate the CBM of each size group separately, multiply by its carton count, then add the group totals.

Is 1 CBM always charged as 1,000 kg?

That is a common sea LCL weight/measure relationship, not a universal rule. Confirm the conversion, rounding and charge basis with the forwarder.

Does a CBM total tell me which container to book?

No. The loading plan also depends on carton shapes, weight, stacking limits, protection and access through the container doors.
