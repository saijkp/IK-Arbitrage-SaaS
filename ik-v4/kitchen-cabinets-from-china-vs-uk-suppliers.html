Kitchen cabinets from China vs UK suppliers

Compare kitchen cabinets from China vs UK suppliers: materials, appliance fit, landed costs, lead times and replacements for UK property projects.

Explore kitchen sourcing

Start with the project, not the factory price

Comparing kitchen cabinets from China vs UK suppliers starts with the installation you need to finish. For a landlord replacing one damaged kitchen, local availability and an easy replacement route may matter more than the unit price. For an HMO owner or developer repeating an approved layout, coordinated factory production may be worth investigating. Neither route is automatically cheaper once the kitchen is fitted.

Prepare one cabinet schedule for both quotes: unit references, dimensions, carcass construction, door finish, hardware, internal fittings, panels, plinths and delivery scope. State whether cabinets arrive assembled or flat packed. A quote for bare boxes cannot be compared fairly with a UK package containing drawers, end panels and fitting support. Ask each supplier to list exclusions before comparing totals.

Specify carcasses and doors separately

Ask what the carcass is made from, its board thickness, edge treatment, backing and fixing method. Plywood, MDF and particleboard describe broad material families, not a complete quality specification. Request the actual board specification and how exposed edges, sink areas and service cut-outs will be protected. Do not accept “waterproof kitchen” as a substitute for a written construction detail.

For doors, distinguish the substrate from the surface: painted MDF, wrapped doors, laminate finishes, veneer and solid timber need different checks. Approve colour, sheen, edge profile, handle drilling and the finish on the reverse. Record acceptable variation in a physical sample, particularly where doors and decorative panels must match.

Specify hinges, drawer runners, loading expectations and adjustment access by identifiable model where possible. For rental properties, a replaceable hinge or runner can be more useful than an unfamiliar decorative feature. Ask the installer to review wall-unit hanging rails, fixings and the proposed assembly instructions. Use the cabinet specification checklist to turn these decisions into an order record.

Check UK appliances and worktops before production

Do not assume that a familiar cabinet width guarantees an appliance will fit. Give the cabinet supplier the exact UK appliance models and manufacturers’ installation drawings. Check oven housing openings, ventilation, fridge door movement, dishwasher door fixing and plinth clearance. Confirm service voids and where pipes, sockets and waste connections need space.

Have the UK installer sign off the dimensioned layout, including finished floor levels, wall irregularities, corner clearances, filler panels and door swings. Check worktop depth, overhang, cabinet support and the positions of sinks and hobs with the worktop fabricator. Decide who templates the worktop after the cabinets are installed; avoid ordering irreversible cut-outs from an unverified room measurement.

A UK cabinet range can also have compatibility limits. Ask both suppliers for the same drawings and a clear list of site adaptations. If importing electrical accessories with the kitchen, request relevant UKCA documentation and model-specific evidence before purchase; confirm applicable product rules using GOV.UK: product conformity and marking guidance. A cabinet drawing alone does not establish electrical product compliance.

Build a landed and installed cost comparison

Use a cost worksheet rather than a claimed percentage saving. Keep cash required for import separate from the final accounting treatment of VAT. Ask your accountant how VAT applies to your business; do not assume every landlord can recover it. Compare these components for the same approved kitchen:

  • Product: cabinets, doors, panels, hardware, spares, samples and protective packaging.
  • Freight: supplier collection, origin handling, sea freight and destination handling; clarify which items are already included.
  • Duty: classification and customs value confirmed for the actual goods, rather than a generic furniture assumption.
  • VAT: import VAT and any separately invoiced services, with the calculation checked by the broker.
  • Delivery: UK transport, access restrictions, unloading and movement from kerbside to the installation area.
  • Installation: assembly, fitting, worktop coordination, adjustments and packaging disposal.
  • Remedial work: missing panels, damaged doors, extra visits and any replacement transport.

Use the correct customs inputs

The commodity code determines the applicable duty treatment; use GOV.UK: finding commodity codes with the kitchen cabinet classification guide. Under the transaction-value method, customs value can include transport and insurance to the UK border; see GOV.UK: customs valuation. Import VAT valuation also includes duty and relevant incidental expenses under GOV.UK: import VAT valuation guidance. The furniture duty and VAT guide explains the wider process. No single rate or factory invoice total replaces a shipment-specific calculation.

Lead times and minimum orders: ask for milestones

Work backwards from the date the installer needs a complete kitchen on site. Allow separately for surveys, drawing revisions, sample approval, production, inspection, collection, shipping, customs arrangements and final delivery. The shipment is only one stage. A late finish approval can move the entire programme even if freight is available.

Ask the Chinese factory and UK supplier to state whether stock is allocated, production is booked or a date is merely indicative. Request milestones and the action taken when one slips. Use the shipping and project lead-time guide when planning the import route, then confirm a current schedule for your order.

There is no universal minimum order for Chinese kitchen factories. Ask whether minimums apply by cabinet, door style, colour or production batch, and whether mixed layouts are accepted. Repeated kitchens may make an order easier to organise, but do not buy unnecessary units simply to meet a production condition. Compare the actual usable order and its storage needs.

Samples and inspection reduce different risks

Approve a door sample and relevant board or hardware samples before releasing production. Label them with the supplier, finish reference and approval date, and keep the agreed reference available for inspection. The sample ordering guide explains what to request and record. A colour seen on a screen is a weak basis for resolving a finish dispute.

Agree an inspection checklist covering dimensions, quantities, finish matching, hardware, assembly trials and packaging. Identify which cabinets or components should be opened and measured. Inspection before dispatch gives you a chance to resolve discrepancies while goods remain with the supplier; carton receiving checks alone do not prove that every cabinet matches the drawing.

Agree replacements before you need them

Ask who handles a damaged door, missing hinge or incorrect panel, what evidence they need and who pays for the part and transport. Record warranty terms and exclusions in the order agreement. Keep finish references, drawings, hardware model numbers and a labelled parts list so future replacements can be identified.

Consider ordering agreed spare hinges, runners or a matching door where justified by the project. Confirm whether the factory will retain the finish specification and accept small replacement orders later. A UK supplier may offer a simpler local collection or replacement process, but check availability rather than assuming it. After-sales arrangements belong in the comparison alongside the initial price.

When a UK supplier is the better choice

Choose a UK supplier when a small one-off job needs a modest number of readily available units, the deadline is very short, or room measurements may still change. Local access to a replacement panel and an installer familiar with the range can outweigh a lower factory quote. This is especially relevant when a delay would leave a tenanted property without a usable kitchen.

China sourcing is worth assessing when layouts and finishes are settled, quantities justify the coordination and the programme allows inspection and shipping. Frequently asked questions

Are kitchen cabinets from China always cheaper than UK suppliers?

No. Compare the same specification through freight, duty, VAT, delivery, installation and remedial work before deciding.

Can imported cabinets fit UK appliances?

They can if the cabinet drawings are checked against the exact appliance installation drawings and approved by your installer before production.

When should I choose a UK kitchen supplier?

A UK supplier may be better for a small one-off job, a very short deadline or a project that needs easy access to replacement parts.
