HMO Room Packs: How to Spec a Standard Room Once

An HMO room pack is one agreed list of furniture and fittings for a standard let bedroom. Write it carefully once, and every refurbishment, replacement and new property can be ordered from the same document.

HMO room pack: quick answer

A standard HMO bedroom pack usually covers sleeping, storage, a place to work and the basic fittings: bed frame, mattress, wardrobe, drawers or bedside unit, desk and chair, mirror, window dressing and lighting. Size it for your smallest regular room, record every dimension, material, finish and compliance document on one schedule, and give the pack a reference so it can be repeated without being re-specified.

Why spec the room once

Most HMO landlords buy furniture in bursts: a refurbishment, a new acquisition, a room that needs replacing between tenancies. If every order starts from a fresh search, the rooms drift apart. Wardrobes end up in three finishes, mattresses come with different paperwork, and nobody can find the right replacement handle.

A fixed room pack solves that. It gives you a consistent standard across the portfolio, a quicker quotation each time and a record of what is in every room. It also makes inspection easier, because the factory and the inspector are checking against the same approved schedule rather than a chain of messages.

What goes in a standard HMO room

Start with what a tenant needs day to day, then decide what your licence conditions, local standards and target tenants add. A typical core pack includes:

  • Bed frame: confirm the bed size for the room, slat spacing, centre support on wider frames and whether you want under-bed storage.
  • Mattress: matched to the frame size, with BS 7177 evidence and a removable or wipe-clean cover if that suits your turnaround routine.
  • Wardrobe: freestanding or fitted, with hanging height, shelf count and whether doors need a lock.
  • Drawers or bedside unit: one or the other in small rooms; both where space allows.
  • Desk and chair: useful for students and professionals working from home. Check the chair if it has upholstery, as it may need a permanent fire label.
  • Mirror, shelf and hooks: small items that tenants notice when they are missing.
  • Window dressing and lighting: blind or curtain sizes taken from the actual windows, plus any ceiling fitting, desk lamp or bedside light.

Keep shared kitchens, lounges and bathrooms on separate schedules. They are bought differently and should not be hidden inside a per-room figure. Our guide to how much it costs to furnish an HMO shows how to budget those areas alongside the bedrooms.

Size the pack for your smallest room

Measure several rooms across the portfolio before fixing any dimension. Record wall lengths, ceiling height, window and radiator positions, sockets, door swing and any chimney breast or boxed-in pipework. A pack that fits your smallest regular room will fit most of the others.

Where rooms vary a lot, create two versions rather than one compromise. For example, a compact pack with a narrower wardrobe and no separate drawers, and a larger pack with both. Keep the same finishes and hardware across versions so replacements stay interchangeable.

Check the delivery route as well as the room. A wardrobe that fits the alcove may not go up a tight staircase in one piece. Flat-pack or knock-down construction is often the practical answer for terraced HMOs, but the factory needs to know before production starts.

Write the specification line by line

For each item, record enough detail that two factories quoting the same line would be quoting the same product. A useful schedule row covers:

  • Room pack reference and item number
  • Overall width, depth and height, plus any internal dimensions that matter
  • Board type and thickness, edging and back panel
  • Finish, colour reference and handle style
  • Hardware: hinges, drawer runners, locks and fixings
  • Assembly: flat-pack, knock-down or fully assembled
  • Compliance evidence required for that item
  • Carton labelling: property, room and item number

Build in durability where tenants use it hardest

HMO furniture is moved, overloaded and cleaned more often than furniture in a family home. Put your durability decisions on the schedule so they cannot be quietly changed:

  • Edges and corners: state the edging on every visible and exposed edge, not just the fronts.
  • Drawers and doors: specify the runner and hinge type rather than accepting the cheapest default.
  • Bed frames: ask how the frame joins and how the slats are fixed, as loose fittings are a common source of complaints.
  • Surfaces: choose finishes that clean easily and hide light scuffs. Avoid glass tops and high-gloss finishes in student rooms unless you are happy to replace them.
  • Fixings: plan wall fixings for tall wardrobes and agree who supplies them.

Ask for a sample of the board, edging and finish before approving the pack. A sample held against the specification is much easier to reorder against later.

Keep fire safety and paperwork in the pack

Compliance belongs on the schedule, not in a separate folder you look for when a council officer asks. For each relevant item, record what evidence you expect from the factory and keep it with the order.

  • Mattresses and beds: evidence of testing to BS 7177.
  • Upholstered furniture such as padded headboards and upholstered chairs: permanent fire labels where the item is covered by the furniture fire safety rules.
  • Lamps and other electrical goods: UKCA or CE conformity documents.

Which items are covered, and what the labels must show, depends on the product. Check the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 and the government guidance on the regulations, and confirm any licence conditions with your local authority before ordering. Plan spares and replacements from day one

The real value of a fixed pack shows up a year later, when a drawer front is broken or a mattress needs replacing. Order a small stock of the parts that wear or go missing: handles, hinges, runners, bed slats, shelf pins and fixings. Keep the finish reference and a photo of the approved sample with the schedule.

When you reorder, quote the pack reference and item number. If a finish or board is no longer available, ask for the proposed substitute to be sampled before it goes into production, so new rooms still match the old ones.

Repeat the pack across properties

Once the pack is approved, each new property needs only a room count, the pack version per room and the delivery details. Label cartons by property and room so the installation team can unload straight to the right floor.

Standard items typically take 10–12 weeks to site; bespoke items 12–18 weeks from approved drawings. Work backwards from the date rooms must be ready, and leave time for assembly, snagging and cleaning after delivery.
