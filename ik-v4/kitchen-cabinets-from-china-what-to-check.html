Kitchen Cabinets from China: What to Check Before You Order

A kitchen quote is only comparable when the specification behind it is written down. Check the board, edging, hardware, worktops and sizes before you pay a deposit, and most surprises on delivery disappear.

Start with a cabinet schedule, not a kitchen price

A price for "a kitchen" tells you very little. Two quotes can look similar and describe different products. Ask for a cabinet schedule that lists every unit by width, depth and height, with the door style, finish and hardware for each line.

Include fillers, end panels, plinths, cornice and any tall units. These are the lines most often missing from a headline price, and they are the ones you notice when the kitchen is being fitted. Our kitchen cabinet sourcing page explains how we price against a schedule.

Carcass board: what the box is made of

The carcass is the box that holds everything else. Common options are plywood, moisture-resistant MDF and melamine-faced particleboard (MFC). They behave differently around water, screws and weight, and they cost different amounts.

  • Ask which board is quoted for the carcass, the back panel and the shelves separately. They are not always the same.
  • Ask for the thickness of each. A thinner shelf or back panel can look identical in a drawing.
  • Think about where the board will sit. Sink units and units beside a dishwasher see far more moisture than a wall cabinet.

If you want to compare options, ask for the same layout priced in two carcass materials, so the difference is the board and nothing else.

Doors, panels and edging

Door construction and finish matter more to the look and life of a kitchen than almost anything else. Confirm whether the door is solid, veneered, laminated, wrapped or painted, and ask for a sample of the exact finish you have chosen.

Look closely at the edges. Exposed cut edges on a carcass are where moisture gets in. Ask how the edges of doors and carcass parts are sealed, which edging is used and how thick it is. Check the inside of a sample cabinet as well as the front.

Hinges, runners and handles

Hardware is where a repeated-use kitchen, such as a rental or a holiday let, wears first. Ask for the hinge and runner type for each line of the schedule, whether doors and drawers close softly, and what weight the drawers are designed to carry.

Also check how the handles are fixed and whether the drilling positions are included in the door price. A handle chosen late can mean a door drilled twice. If handles are supplied separately, put them on the schedule as their own line.

Worktops, sinks and appliances

Worktops are usually a separate item from the cabinets, and the cut-outs for the hob and sink have to match the exact products you are buying. Give the supplier your appliance model numbers and the sink and tap dimensions before the drawings are approved.

Confirm who is responsible for each of these:

  • worktop material, thickness and edge profile;
  • cut-outs for the hob, sink and any tap holes;
  • upstands and splashbacks;
  • appliance ventilation gaps and integrated appliance door fixings.

Unless a line is on your quotation, assume it is excluded. Site survey, fitting, plumbing and electrics are not part of a sourcing order unless they are listed.

Sizes: measure before you draw

Most kitchen problems on site start with measurements. Measure each wall at several heights, because walls are rarely straight and floors are rarely level. Record ceiling height, window and door positions, radiators, and the position of water, waste, gas and electrical points.

Then check the drawing against the room, not just against the schedule. Make sure the run of cabinets plus fillers actually fits the wall, that doors and drawers can open fully past each other at corners, and that tall units clear any bulkhead or sloping ceiling.

Approve drawings and samples before production

Final shop drawings should be signed off by one named person before manufacture starts. A late change to a cabinet width or door finish can affect several components, so ask what a change will do to the programme before you agree to it.

Ask for a finish sample and, where it matters, a corner sample showing the carcass, edging and hardware. Our design and specification service covers layouts, mood boards and product schedules, prepared by our in-house designer for a fixed fee agreed in advance and credited against the order.

Check the paperwork around the cabinets

Cabinets are only part of a kitchen order. If you are importing lighting, extractors or other electrical goods, ask for the UKCA or CE conformity documents. Duty and VAT depend on what you import and how it is classified, so confirm the commodity code on the UK Trade Tariff before relying on any figure, and see our import duty and VAT guide for how to plan the budget.

Timing and payment

Standard items typically take 10 to 12 weeks to site. Bespoke items typically take 12 to 18 weeks from approved drawings, so the drawings are the milestone to protect. Our guide to shipping times shows how production, inspection, freight and delivery fit together.

The sourcing fee is 5% of product value and is shown on every quotation. A 50% deposit places the order, 30% follows factory video confirmation, and 20% is due on delivery. There is no minimum to enquire.

A short checklist before you pay a deposit

  1. A cabinet schedule with every unit, filler, panel and plinth.
  2. Carcass, back panel and shelf board named, with thicknesses.
  3. Door construction and finish confirmed against a sample.
  4. Edging described for doors and carcass.
  5. Hinge, runner and handle types on each line.
  6. Worktop, sink and appliance cut-outs matched to real model numbers.
  7. Measured plans checked against the drawings, and one named approver.
  8. Exclusions written down: fitting, plumbing, electrics and anything else not listed.
